Section 1 · Overview
Reusing D2D Infrastructure for GPS-Independent PNT
Any LEO direct-to-device (D2D) system operating in licensed S-band mobile-satellite service (MSS) spectrum ends up with a small amount of unused guard-band margin at the edge of its channel — a byproduct of fitting a standardized NR channel bandwidth inside a slightly larger raw spectrum license. This note works through a concrete design for putting that margin to use: a continuous-wave (CW) pilot tone already needed for D2D acquisition and power-saving, extended into a standalone GNSS-independent positioning, navigation, and timing (PNT) capability — at near-zero incremental spectrum or hardware cost.

Why this is worth doing
  • De-risks standardization: works even if in-band CW proposals never reach 3GPP consensus
  • Near-zero cost: reuses the same low-power CW monitor already needed for D2D acquisition/DRX
  • Genuine market: resilient/alternative PNT is a fast-growing, policy-backed category (Section 6)
  • System-level payoff: the satellite operator's own infrastructure (orbit determination, ground timing) often depends on GNSS too
Section 2 · Guard-Band Feasibility
Why Licensed Spectrum Has Unused Margin at All
3GPP NR only defines a discrete set of standardized channel bandwidths per numerology — there is no channel bandwidth option matching an arbitrary raw spectrum license. Real MSS-adjacent licenses in this frequency range are often a few MHz wider than any single standard NR channel, which is precisely what creates usable margin.

Lower license edge—Upper license edge (adjacent licensee)
Standard 3GPP NR channel bandwidths at 15kHz SCS (TS 38.101-1 / 38.104): 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50 MHz A raw license of ~16-17 MHz does not match any of these exactly. Rounding UP to the next standard size (e.g. 20 MHz) would exceed the license and risk interference into the adjacent licensee's spectrum. Rounding DOWN to 15 MHz fits comfortably, leaving ~1-1.5 MHz margin.
Guard bands are a standard, regulator-endorsed interference tool

This is not a novel workaround — regulators in this frequency range have historically created explicit guard-band allocations of their own to separate MSS spectrum from adjacent mobile broadband allocations, precisely because out-of-band emissions (OOBE) are highest immediately adjacent to a transmit channel and decay with frequency separation. A guard band gives that natural roll-off room to happen before reaching a protected neighboring licensee — and a narrow, tightly power-controlled CW tone is a far gentler occupant of that space than trying to squeeze additional wideband data traffic into it.

Engineering consequence: less guard margin forces a stricter close-in emission mask, which in turn demands sharper output filtering and/or more aggressive PA linearization (backoff, predistortion) to suppress spectral regrowth near the boundary. More guard margin relaxes that requirement. The 1-1.5 MHz recovered here is effectively a substitute for some of that filtering/linearity effort — and putting a CW tone there, rather than leaving it idle, gets productive use out of spectrum that would otherwise sit completely unused.

Section 4 · Signal Design
A GPS-Style Ranging Code Within <1.5 MHz
CW alone gives Doppler-only positioning (the classic 1960s Transit satnav approach) — workable, but slow, needing minutes of pass tracking per fix. Adding a spread-spectrum ranging code, GPS-style, upgrades this to fast, direct time-of-flight positioning.
Ranging code parameters
GPS L1 C/A reference: 1.023 Mchip/s → 2.046 MHz (too wide) Designed for guard band: 0.60 Mchip/s → 1.20 MHz (fits) Chip length: 1.67 microseconds Raw code resolution: ~500 m before tracking refinement (GPS itself starts from a similar order chip length and refines to meter-level via code + carrier tracking loops) Structure: Code-division (CDMA), not frequency-division All satellites share the same ~1.2MHz ranging slice Each satellite: unique Gold code / PRN sequence (as GPS SVs) Receiver correlates against known PRNs to separate satellites → resolves satellite identity independent of Doppler, a more fundamental fix than frequency-separated CW tones for the zenith Doppler-collision case
Navigation message overlay

GPS's navigation message runs at just 50 bps, BPSK-modulated directly onto the ranging code. The same technique carries ephemeris, clock corrections, and satellite health — at zero additional bandwidth beyond the ranging code itself.

1.5 MHz guard band budget: ~1.2 MHz ranging code (all satellites, CDMA) ~0.3 MHz margin / CW tones alongside 0 MHz nav message (rides on ranging code)

Why constellation density matters here specifically: GPS needs 4 simultaneously visible satellites for a full position+time fix (3, if altitude is already known — common for ground-based use). A dense LEO D2D constellation sized for global messaging coverage typically provides well beyond that minimum at usable elevation angles, which a sparse constellation optimized purely for occasional communication passes would struggle to guarantee. The same density the communication mission needs anyway is what makes fast, accurate ranging-based PNT geometrically realistic as a side capability.

This landscape shows real, validated demand for GNSS-independent PNT across multiple architectures — the guard-band approach in this note is a low-incremental-cost way for any existing LEO D2D operator to participate, riding on infrastructure already built for the communications mission.
The reframe: the strongest case for building this isn't "compete for PNT market share" — it's that an operator's own ground infrastructure and satellite operations (orbit determination, precise timing) often already depend on GNSS today, at a moment when jamming/spoofing is growing sharply. A guard-band PNT signal, riding on infrastructure the D2D mission needs anyway, is close to free insurance against that dependency — with a genuine, already-validated external market as a secondary upside, not the primary justification.
