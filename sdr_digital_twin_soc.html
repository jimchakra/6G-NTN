Methodology
The methodology always worked. AI made it affordable.
The digital-twin flow for silicon was never the problem — its cost was.

The opening. The digital-twin methodology for silicon has always worked. It was simply too expensive to build and keep in sync, demanding a modeling organization most programs could never justify. AI collapses exactly that cost — a small senior team can now execute a program that traditionally required a roughly 300-person organization.

The catch. AI alone does not do it. Modeling and simulation are the work AI accelerates best, but without senior architects who know how to stand up and sustain the flow, it only accelerates the path to a silicon re-spin. The judgment is the part AI does not commoditize.

So the bottleneck moves — from headcount and budget to architectural judgment and flow expertise. That is the opportunity, and the risk when a team is staffed wrong.

Concrete target. Close a multi-mode wireless SoC — for example Ka-band broadband plus S-band direct-to-device — in 2–3 tape-outs, with mass production within 18 months of first tape-out.
Architecture
Four reinforcing pillars
Each reinforces the others; none carries the design alone.
Pillar 1 · SDR
Flexible, serviceable silicon

A waveform-agnostic architecture with programmability only where it matters. It supports concurrent multi-mode operation and post-launch upgrades — decisive for silicon with no field serviceability — and flexes to a changing roadmap instead of freezing at tape-out.

Pillar 2 · Digital Twins
De-risked delivery

High-level (tail, contention, DVFS) and low-level (cycle-accurate, firmware from day one) simulators make first bring-up a confirmation rather than a discovery — the single largest lever against the re-spin.

Pillar 3 · Core IPs
A reusable cornerstone

Proven blocks reused across product lines on one tool chain: the large (MAC/L2) core, the small (PHY/L1) core, the vector engine, the SDR coprocessors. The investment compounds with every program.

Pillar 4 · Low Power
Efficiency by design

Treated as a first-class axis: digital pre-distortion on an Echo State Network (ESN), DVFS gears sized by the high-level simulator, low-power IPs across the datapath, and DRX / WUS for idle-time savings.

The Key Lever
AI as a force multiplier — done right
Why the modeling flow that was always too expensive is suddenly within reach of a small team.

The high- and low-level modeling methodology has been respected for years and rarely practiced, because only well-funded teams could afford the 30–40-person effort it required, and only for high-value, high-volume production.

What changed is the economics. Modeling and simulation are low-risk, low-stakes code where the bar is correctness — not synthesizability, area efficiency, or timing closure, as RTL demands. That is precisely the work AI accelerates best, and it collapses a 30–40-person modeling team to three to five senior engineers.

Re-spin economics follow directly: a program's cost is roughly heads × time × rate. Keeping the tape-out count low by making first bring-up a confirmation, not a discovery, is the whole game.

Where trust bottoms out. The verification specification is the true golden reference. Models and RTL must be checked against it independently — because a correlated error produces a silent false pass, the most dangerous outcome in the flow. AI raises the premium on that independence, since it makes it cheap to generate two implementations that are wrong in the same way. Senior judgment steering the AI is the single most effective lever a silicon team can invest in for long-term stability.
The Digital Twin
Two simulators
One finds the tail; the other brings up the firmware before silicon exists.
High-Level Simulator

What: lightweight Python/C models capturing SW/HW dependency and shared-resource contention — processors, memories, buses, concurrent modes — to drive the design into its tail.

  • RTL simulation is far too slow to reach beyond-P99 tail events; the high-level model runs over 1,000,000× faster and catches them.
  • Right-sizes the design — avoiding both under- and over-provisioning.
  • Surfaces the escape hatches rare tail events need (NACK handling, priority inversion).
  • The indispensable tool for choosing DVFS gears.
Low-Level Simulator

What: a bit-true, approximately cycle-accurate C++/SystemC model of real hardware behavior.

  • Gives software and firmware teams the hardware months before tape-out.
  • Lets verification run real firmware to stress the system 10–100× harder than RTL simulation alone.
  • Turns integration from a post-silicon scramble into a pre-silicon exercise.
Note — P99 is the 99th-percentile (tail) metric. "P99" is used throughout to avoid collision with the 3GPP Release-99 meaning of "R99".
Why SDR, Specifically
SDR for silicon in orbit
Bugs surface after tape-out, and some only in production. Silicon in orbit has no field serviceability, which turns an ordinary bug into a long-term program risk. A waveform-agnostic SDR architecture changes that exposure.

The Deep Architecture
Making SDR practical for OFDM
Pure CPU/DSP SDR is impractical for high-throughput, low-latency, low-cost silicon. The discipline is to keep programmability only where it matters. In OFDM, "flexibility" means two distinct things: how the resource grid is allocated (the scalar plane, on the CPU) and how it is used (the vector plane, on the DSP).
Scalar Plane · Resource-Grid Allocation

3GPP signals PDSCH locations with a bitmap (Type 0, in both LTE and NR), and the pattern generalizes to resource-grid allocation across channels. General-purpose processors are poor at bit manipulation, so the move is to find the operations common across waveforms and build custom instructions or coprocessors for them:

OperationFunction
Expand1→2, 1→4, 1→8 by AL or RBG size
Shiftper BWP (NR) or sub-beam (satellite)
Punctureremove pilot and reserved locations
Mergecombine pilot and data bitmaps
Pack / Unpackcontrol-plane fields
Addr Genthe bitmap drives fetches into IQ / LLR buffers
Vector Plane · Resource-Grid Usage

Not every datapath should be programmable; some sit deep in negative PPA territory if forced into a DSP. Partition by strength. Most of the map is consensus — hardware accelerators for DFE, FFT, and the decoder; DSP for channel/noise estimation, channel selection, and channel mixing.

The architecture lives in the two non-obvious calls.
Demod → HW: demodulation belongs in a hardware accelerator, resisting the pull toward a DSP.
Sync → DSP: synchronization belongs in a DSP, resisting the pull to hard-wire it.
The rest is settled; those two are the ones worth defending.
Multi-Cluster Control Plane · Two Core Types

Modem control planes serve two very different workload profiles, and mixing them compromises performance and stability. The answer is two core types on the same ISA and tool chain, with different micro-architectures.

 MAC / L2 — largePHY / L1 — small
Working setmulti-MB (scheduler, RRC)~10 KB (TTI / slot / beam mgmt, RF control)
Latency tolerance~10 ms — tolerant~10 µs — intolerant
MemoryDDR-backed, large cachetightly-coupled memory, cacheless
Measured ontotal MIPSresponsiveness
Deploymenta handful of large cores at 70–80% utilizationa large farm of small cores below ~30% utilization

Separating them protects modem performance and stability, and it right-sizes PPA: small working sets run on small cores, while the scheduler's loose-latency decision-making runs on the large cores. High-capacity scheduling across beams × panels may call for NN on the large cores.

One architecture, all product lines. The same architecture serves multiple concurrent modes; with different instance counts — processors, DSPs, decoders — it serves both base-station and satellite modems on a single tool chain.
